Rose colours and their meanings: 8 colours explained

Red, pink, white, yellow, orange, peach, lavender and dark red roses: what each colour means and which occasion it fits.

Red roses mean love, pink means gratitude, white means purity or remembrance, yellow means friendship, and orange means enthusiasm. Those five cover most bouquets. The table below adds peach, lavender and dark red, and tells you the occasion each colour fits best, so you can choose quickly and avoid the two classic mistakes: yellow for a lover, red for a boss.

Where these meanings come from

Most of what we "know" about rose colours is a nineteenth-century invention. Victorian Britain and France were fond of floriography, a coded language in which each flower and colour carried a message that could be sent when words were improper. Dozens of dictionaries were published, and they did not always agree with each other; the red rose for love and the white rose for innocence are the most stable entries, while orange and lavender roses barely existed yet and acquired their meanings later, mostly through florists in the twentieth century. Some associations are much older: red roses were linked to Aphrodite and Venus in Greek and Roman writing, and the white rose became a Marian symbol in medieval Christianity.

Combinations and numbers

  • Red and white together traditionally mean unity, which is why they are popular at weddings.
  • Yellow with red tips signals friendship warming into love.
  • A single rose says "you are the one"; a dozen is the classic declaration; two dozen says "I think of you every hour".
  • Blue and black roses do not exist in nature; they are dyed or very dark red. Blue is read as mystery or the unattainable, black as farewell or rebirth, depending on who you ask.

Common questions

Which colour for a first date? Pink or peach: warm without pressure. For a sympathy bouquet? White, sometimes pale pink; yellow if the person was a close friend. For a colleague? Yellow, peach or orange; never red alone. Does the number of roses really matter? Only as much as you want it to; the colour sends the louder signal.

Sources: Victorian and Edwardian flower-language dictionaries, classical and medieval references to the rose, and modern florist trade guides.
